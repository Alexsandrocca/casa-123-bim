No. The pipe would reach the sewer at or below its bottom. A side connection has to come in above the sewer pipe, so gravity only works if SEMAE confirms the sewer is at least about 3.4 m deep in front of number 123.
  • Lower WC and laundry: a sealed lift station (a pit with a pump, an alarm and a check valve) pumps up to the gravity line under the street floor. It's small, it sits in the lower WC, and it runs on the backup-power circuit.
  • Street floor and upper floor: gravity at 2 % in a 100 mm pipe, with a grease trap for the kitchen and an inspection box at the property line. They reach the sewer at about −2.7, above the pipe.
  • Backflow valve: every fixture below street level gets one, so the street sewer can never back up into the house.
  • Water: the main is 1.50 m deep. The meter at the front wall feeds 2 × 1,000 L tanks on the roof over the stair (1–2 days for 5 people). Everything is fed from the tanks, with a small pressure pump for the upstairs showers. Hot water comes from a 300 L heat-pump water heater running on the solar power.

Rainwater (separate 100 mm drainage)

rainwater, gravityrainwater, pumped
  • Rainwater never goes into the sewer. By law the two networks stay separate, and in this plan they share no pipe.
  • Roofs (about 150 m²): 100 mm downpipes feed a 5,000 L cistern under the front setback. It supplies garden watering, WC flushing and the laundry through a separate non-potable line (NBR 15527). Overflow goes by gravity to the street gutter at the curb.
  • Garden and lower terrace (−2.55) sit below the street and below the neighbour at the rear. They drain into a lawn infiltration trench first. A sump with a float pump sends heavy storms up to the cistern or the gutter.
  • Downpipes and horizontal runs are 100 mm with at least 1 % fall. Gutters are sized for Piracicaba's rainfall per NBR 10844 in the detailed design.

Electrical, panels and solar

  • Supply: three-phase from the local utility (CPFL in Piracicaba; confirm the voltage and the entry standard with them). The meter is bidirectional for solar credits.
  • Main panel in the garage: surge protection, 30 mA RCDs on every circuit group (NBR 5410), a per-circuit energy meter, and breakers or relays you can switch from the app. There's a sub-panel on the lower level for the studio, pumps and the rack.
  • Solar: 12 × 550 W = 6.6 kWp on the upper roof, tilted 20° to the north. That's roughly 750–800 kWh a month, about what a family of 5 uses with air-conditioning, induction cooking, a heat-pump water heater and an electric car. The garage roof takes 6 more modules later.
  • Battery (optional, 10 kWh): keeps lights, fridge, internet, cameras and pumps running in a blackout. The lift pump and the garden sump must never stop, so they're on this circuit.
  • Controls: a home automation hub with smart switches (Matter/Zigbee), app control of the air-conditioning and water heater, and alerts for the pumps and the tank levels.
